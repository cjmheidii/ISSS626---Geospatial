Overview

STILL A WORK IN PROGRESS (WIP)

Since gaining independence from the UK in 1948, Myanmar has been in one of the world’s longest ongoing civil war. Ethic armed organisations have been fighting Myanmar’s armed forces for self-determination. Even with the numerous ceasefires, armed groups continue to call for independence, increased autonomy, democracy, or the federalisation of Myanmar.

In February 2021, the military of Myanmar has taken over the country after overthrowing the 2020 elected government that is supposed by the National League for Democracy. However instead of restoring order like many had hoped the Junta’s repression followed with brutal crackdowns instead. This caused millions of civilians to abandon the peaceful protests to take up arms or seek refuge in dense forests and mountainous borderlands. Consequently, the country has descended into an all-out civil war that has claimed thousands of lives, displaced over three million people, and fractured Myanmar into competing zones of control, leaving vast stretches of territory entirely outside the junta’s reach.

Objectives

This Take-Home Exercise explores the usage of Local Measures of Spatial Association methods and Emerging Hot Spot Analysis to identify, assess and geovisualizing the spatial distribution, patterns and trends of armed conflict in the Myanmar at township level from 1st January 2021 to 30th September 2025.

The specific objectives of this take-home exercise are:

  • To pinpoint exact geographic hotspots, cold spots, and spatial outliers where violence clusters or behaves unusually compared to surrounding townships.

  • To reveal where, how fast, and in what pattern political violence is evolving over space and time.

This is to assist in the overall objectives on peace-building initiatives and preventing further armed conflicts in the country.

Data

These are the datasets used for this exercise

Geospatial Data

Myanmar Township Boundaries MIMU v9.4 is a geospatial dataset that delineates the boundaries of Myanmar. This data is presented in the ESRI shapefile format and is based on the WGS 84 geographic coordinate system. This data is extracted from MIMU Geonode.

Aspatial Data

Armed Conflict Location & Event Data Project (ACLED) will be used. This is an aspatial tabular dataset (CSV) that records political violence and conflict events across Myanmar from January 2021 to September 2025.Geographic point coordinate attributes (latitude and longitude in decimal degrees) are incorporated for spatial referencing. This data is is taken from SMU’s E-LEARN Site.

Creating SpaceTime Cube

Within the sfdep package, to be able to represent the spatio-temporal data for the Geospatial analysis and Emerging HotSpot Analysis (EHSA) , the spacetime class would be used since it is able to hold both the spatial and temporal aspects of the data required.

So to create a spacetime object four items are required :

  • Data : tibble of conflict counts

  • geometry : an sf object of township boundaries (mmr_sf)

  • location identifier : Column in both data in geometry (TS_PCODE)

  • Time : Month index in data to as a column holding the temporal based data.

In here, every township should appear in every month, including township without any conflicts to ensure the cube is complete. The 4 steps below would help to create the cube :

Creating Time unit

First the time unit of the months would be created since the Time factor as stated above is required to create the spacetime cube.

acled_ts <- acled_ts %>%
  mutate(month = floor_date(event_date, "month"),
         t_idx = interval(ymd("2021-01-01"), month) %/% months(1) + 1)

range(acled_ts$t_idx)   
[1]  1 57

Since we know there are 57 months output should be 1, 57. Which is the case so there is no issues with this first step and can proceed on to step 2.

No of conflict event by Township & Month

The code below will count by Month (Temporal) and Township (Spatial) the number of conflicts that had occurred in the 57 months.

counts <- acled_ts %>%
  st_drop_geometry() %>%
  group_by(TS_PCODE, t_idx) %>%
  summarise(n_events = n(), fatalities = sum(fatalities), .groups = "drop")

Accounting for Zeros

Townships with no events in a month will not appear in counts. So an additional step is required to add these township back with zero counts.

cube_df <- counts %>%
  complete(TS_PCODE = mmr_sf$TS_PCODE,
           t_idx = 1:57,
           fill = list(n_events = 0, fatalities = 0))

nrow(cube_df) == nrow(mmr_sf) * 57   
[1] TRUE

Create spacetime object

Finally now the spacetime object can be created.

acled_st <- spacetime(cube_df, mmr_sf,
                      .loc_col  = "TS_PCODE",
                      .time_col = "t_idx")

is_spacetime_cube(acled_st)   
[1] TRUE

The TRUE Confirms the creation of the spacetime cube!

Assumptions & Limitations in data

CRS on Both Datasets

The EPSG:4326 is used to importing and storing raw latitude/longitude points for both aspatial and geospatial datasets. However this is not suitable for the spatial analytics in this exercise across Myanmar. Hence a transformation is required to EPSG:32647 (UTM Zone 47N). This is done during the importing of the datasets st_transform(crs = 32647)

First a reaggregation is needed to calculate out the fatality counts to plot, done usingt the code below.

# Re-aggregation of event types for fatality counts
ts_agg <- acled_ts %>%
  st_drop_geometry() %>%
  group_by(TS_PCODE) %>%
  summarise(
    n_events       = n(),

Task 2 : Local Measures of Spatial Association (LMSA) Analysis

Now, we will move on to the LMSA analysis, which focuses on the relationships between each observation and its surroundings to allow us to learn more about the spatial structure in the Myanmar conflict and its data.

First the Queen Contiguity is used for the Spatial Weights. This is because the Queens Contiguity will define neighbors as polygons that share either a common edge OR a single corner point (vertex) unlike the other available contiguity (Rook) which only looks at the shared boundary line of non-zero length.

Situation Rook Neighbour Queen Neighbour
Share a full edge ✅ ✅
Share only a single corner point ❌ ✅
Share nothing ❌ ❌
Touch at a single point (diagonal) ❌ ✅

LISA for Conflict Frequency

Computing local Moran’s I

To compute local Moran’s I, the local_moran() function of sfdep will be used to compute the local Gi*. It will compute mainly the following :

  • Ii: the local Moran’s I statistics

  • E.Ii: the expectation of local moran statistic under the randomisation hypothesis

  • Var.Ii: the variance of local moran statistic under the randomisation hypothesis

  • Z.Ii:the standard deviate of local moran statistic

  • Pr(): the p-value of local moran statistic

Bonus Analysis on unusually deadly Conflicts

We have analysed how often violence occurs (events) and of the fatalities from the armed conflicts. However there is no indication on how deadly each incident is. To achieve peace-building needs, there is a need for an integrated understanding on where violence is frequent, and where it is unusually lethal per incident.

So now the investigation here will look at which townships have unusually deadly violence, and do they match the event hot spots found earlier? It goes beyond the core methods by using empirical Bayes rate smoothing, which is not covered in class.

The code below calculates raw lethality for each township (deaths / events) and plots it against the number of events. The red dashed line is the national rate and the red curves show the range expected by chance alone. This checks whether raw lethality can be trusted before it is used in LISA. Townships with no events are set to 0 in this case because their rate is undefined.

wm_q_eb <- wm_q %>%
  mutate(raw_rate = if_else(n_events > 0, n_fatalities / n_events, 0))

# National rate, how many average deaths per event conflict
b <- sum(wm_q_eb$n_fatalities) / sum(wm_q_eb$n_events)

# From b, create a 5% confidence interval (funnel by poisson)
funnel <- tibble(n_events = exp(seq(log(1), log(1100), length.out = 200))) %>%
  mutate(upper = b + 1.96 * sqrt(b / n_events),
         lower = pmax(b - 1.96 * sqrt(b / n_events), 0))

#plot
ggplot(wm_q_eb %>% filter(n_events > 0),
       aes(x = n_events, y = raw_rate)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.6) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = b, linetype = "dashed", colour = "red") +
  geom_line(data = funnel, aes(y = upper), colour = "red") +
  geom_line(data = funnel, aes(y = lower), colour = "red") +
  scale_x_log10() +
  labs(x = "Events (log scale)", y = "Raw fatalities per event",
       title = "When there are few events, raw lethality is unstable",
       subtitle = "Dashed line = national rate; red curves = range expected from chance alone") +
  theme_minimal()
print(b)
[1] 1.652681
Note

Most of the armed conflict counts in a very narrow band within the funnel plot, but there are outliers where a township with one or two events can show a rate of 3 or 0 purely by chance from the left side of the funnel plot.

Therefore, running LISA for this portion on raw ratios can be misleading since noisy small townships would produce extreme values that skew their neighbours’ local averages, creating false High-High or Low-Low clusters and false outliers.

This is proven further by comparing the red funnel where b=1.65, and many points are outside of the funnel range. Therefore, Empirical Bayes smoothing is done to correct this by shrinking each township’s rate toward the national rate. This is so that the differences that survive smoothing are more likely to reflect genuinely deadlier violence than small-number noise.

As a note, with b = 1.65, it means that for every recorded armed conflict in Myanmar, there are about 1.65 deaths.

Need to write an exp for the code chunk below

s2 <- sum(wm_q_eb$n_events * (wm_q_eb$raw_rate - b)^2) / sum(wm_q_eb$n_events)
a  <- max(s2 - b / mean(wm_q_eb$n_events), 0)             

wm_q_eb <- wm_q_eb %>%
  mutate(eb_w    = a / (a + b / n_events),                # weight on the township's own rate
         eb_rate = b + eb_w * (raw_rate - b))

The code below smooths each township’s rate. With b=1.65 being generated, and a being the township variance, the township weight (eb_w) is calculated where value should be between 0 and 1: close to 1 for townships with many armed conflict events, close to 0 for those with few.

The smoothed rate (eb_rate) will then blend the township’s own rate with the national rate using that weight. The output, eb_rate, replaces the raw ratio in every step that follows. The smoothing will remove the skewing issues as seen in previous visual.

Note

Only 6 of the 49 event hot spots (about 12%) are lethal clusters as well (red), where all of them are at the western side of the central-northwest hot spot. These regions are known to have frequent armed conflicts, where all of them are very deadly as well, the most dangerous townships to be in during the 57 months.

The other 43 (yellow) are busy but not unusually deadly per incident, including the north-central patch and every south-eastern hot spot in Mon, Kayin and Tanintharyi. This suggests the south-east corridor’s high fatalities is due to the volume of events, rather than it being deadly incidents.

Fifteen townships (blue) are lethal clusters outside any event hot spot. They ring the north and west of the main hot spot. These are clusters that are affected as a consequence to armed conflicts in the yellow or red area, which shows that there are quite a number of townships where peace is affected due to neighbouring regions.

Assumption

Deaths per event are treated as Poisson-like, but real counts vary more, so the smoothing strength is approximate. Events are the exposure, so results show lethality per incident, not risk per resident. Shrinkage pulls every township towards one national rate, even though the funnel plot shows lethality rising with event volume, so small townships in very deadly areas may be understated. ACLED fatality figures are reported estimates and may be undercounted. Clusters depend on the 5% threshold across about 327 simultaneous tests, with no multiple-testing adjustment. Results describe spatial association, not causes.
