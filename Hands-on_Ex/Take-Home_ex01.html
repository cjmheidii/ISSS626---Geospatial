Overview

Public safety refers to the protection of the general public, communities, and critical infrastructure from crimes, disasters, accidents, and other major threats through coordinated government and agency action.

Geospatial analytics, supported by multi-source geospatial data and geospatial data science and analysis methods, provides powerful means to transform location-based data into visual evidence, analytical insights, and decision support. It plays an important role in areas such as law enforcement, emergency management, disaster response, transportation safety, and environmental management.

A spatial point pattern represents a collection of geographic locations at which particular events occur, such as forest-fire detections or road traffic accidents. A spatio-temporal point pattern extends this representation by incorporating the time at which each event occurs, allowing analysts to investigate how the distribution and interaction of events vary across both space and time.

Understanding where and when public-safety events occur can help planners and managers identify areas of concern, understand patterns of event occurrence, allocate resources, and develop evidence-informed strategies for prevention and response.

Objectives

This take-home exercise investigates the spatial and/or spatio-temporal patterns of public-safety point events using one the case study of Road traffic accidents within the Thailand with emphasis on the Bangkok Metropolitan Region (BMR) for the selected study year 2022.

In Thailand, it is known that they have recorded one of the highest road death tolls in the world, which emphasizes the importance of having this analysis within the exercise to gain proper insights to help make better decisions and actionable(s) to reduce the accident rates in the country and in lieu the death toll.

The specific objectives from this exercise are :

  • To prepare and integrate real-world geospatial and temporal data for spatial and spatio-temporal point-pattern analysis.

  • To investigate first-order properties of the point pattern by examining how the intensity or distribution of events varies across space and/or time.

  • To investigate second-order properties by examining whether events exhibit evidence of spatial or spatio-temporal interaction, such as clustering, inhibition, or approximate randomness.

  • To critically evaluate the analytical assumptions, methodological choices, and limitations associated with the selected point-pattern methods.

  • To communicate the analytical findings effectively through appropriate geovisualisation and geocommunication.

  • To translate the analytical evidence into meaningful implications for public-safety planning and management.

Assumptions & Decisions made

Assumptions made

Some assumptions made in this exercise for the analysis to be valid. The list is as follows

Reliability of original Dataset

There is always the possibility of human error, where in this case human error can refer to personnel forgetting to record an accident that has occurred. So we will assume here that the data provided is fully representative of all accidents that occurred in 2022.

Homogeneity

It is assumed that the risk of every accident occuring across Thailand is uniform, or exactly the same. In reality, there are many variables that can influence this risk level like the volume of traffic or level of maintenance of the roads or etc. This assumption will be put to allow exploratory trend detection.

Independence

We assume that each accident is independent of each other. There can be times in reality where an accident can occur due to the occurrence of another, this is known as multi-vehicle crashes. Hence multiple points in this case will be recorded within these datasets, but we will assume in this exercise that all accidents are recorded as separate events.

Isotropy

We assume here that the intensity of the accidents is the same in all directions. In reality, road networks are linear, so accidents are constrained to roads. So with the assumption, the analysis can be a relatively useful form of visualisation on risk.

Decisions made

The Coordinate Reference System (CRS) was decided to be changed within the Thailand Boundary from WGS 84 (EPSG:4326) to UTM Zone 47N (EPSG:32647). This is essential because UTM coordinates are in meters, which allows distance-based methods (bandwidth selection, Ripley’s K, edge correction) to operate on meaningful planar distances rather than degrees.

Task 2: First-Order Point Pattern Analysis

Computing accident STKDE by Month

What we currently have is only the overall accident distribution in the entire year of 2022, but we cannot see where and when the accidents would occur together.

With the STKDE by month helping to estimate a 3D intensity surface on 1 plot per month for a total of 12 plots, it gives a smoothed continuous surface to help compare the intensity of accidents. We can see which areas within Thailand have a high accident intensity and in which months the intensity is concentrated.

Therefore STKDE by month is done for the 1st Order Point Analysis because it provides the insights on the where and when the accident happens together, or the analysis of both spatial and temporal dimensions simultaneously.

It is known that a larger h produces a smoother, more generalised surface while a smaller h reveals local hot spots. The temporal bandwidth is estimated to be lambda = 0.0079 (in units of the temporal range). Since time was rescaled to [1, 12] months, we have a narrow temporal smoothing window of about 0.1 month, which means that the density surface will change sharply month-to-month which is best to emphasize the high intensity time scope of April and December. The trivariate density is normalised and conditioned to allow each monthly slice to integrate to 1 and make for direct visual comparison across months.

Task 3: Second-Order Point Pattern Analysis

Now, since we had identified that the Bangkok Metropolitan Region, North Eastern Region and North Thailand as the hot-spots for accidents to occur in Thailand, we will screen in to analyse more deeply within this region to get the most impact out of the analysis in preventing future accidents on the road.

For this Second Order Point Pattern Analysis, we will be utilising the K and L Functions. The reason is as stated below.

Clark Evans only looks at a one distance to the nearest point, which computes R and returns a p value for significance at a very short range scale (nearest neighbour distance). However, the K Function and L Function helps to provide a better idea on how clustering varies with distance which the previous Clark-Evans test does not provide.

Previously we learnt in Thailand Is there clustering at all? Now as a research question we want to understand at which distance does this clustering occur now that we had proven the existance of the clustering at these 3 main regions : Bangkok Metropolitan Region, North Eastern Region and North Thailand.

K - Function

In the previous task 2, the Clark-Evans test has established that the accidents does cluster significantly in all 3 highlighted regions, but this is only proven at the nearest neighbour scale. Therefore, for the second order analysis the Ripley’s K Function is conducted to get an understanding on the clustering across the full range of spatial scales. Specifically (even though there is also the F and G Function), the K function allows every distance of clustering to be captured to the rmax so every event (denoted as accident in this scenario) up to the rmax can be measured, which helps to identify the spatial scales where the accident is concentrating which is what is required for the objective of the exercise which is to better plan for road safety.

Considerations & Assumptions made

Rmax Consideration for K function calculation

Before the K function is calculated, we look at the rmax parameter. This is the parameter that sets the maximum inter-point distance at which Ripley’s K is evaluated, and is important to evaluate because Ripley’s edge correction would be unstable as the evaluation distance grows which is the case for our study where we are looking at large regions within Thailand.

Usually, the default rmax is already derived from the bounding rectangle of the window so the code chunk below is not necessary. However for our scenario, it is necessary to compute the estimated rmax parameters since our dataset has a multipolygon which does overstate the region effective extent. This is for more accurate results and faster computation within R studio. The computed distances below represent the upper bound where K can be reliably interpreted for each of the regions.

# For each region, look at the window's extent in km
rmax_rule <- function(ppp) {
  W <- Window(ppp)
  x_extent <- diff(W$xrange)   # in km
  y_extent <- diff(W$yrange)   # in km
  rmax <- min(x_extent, y_extent) / 4
  c(x_extent = x_extent, y_extent = y_extent, rmax = rmax)
}

Limitations/Caveats

Reduction of nrval and subsampling in specified regions

The Bangkok Metropolitian region analysis will uses the standard nrval (100) and subsampling (1000) values , which is well-suited to the renrvallatively compact BMR window of rmax =23.58km. However, the Northern and North-Eastern regions with larger, more elongated boundary windows that are bounded by more complex administrative boundaries, the isotropised correction would be computationally complex to run within R studio with the standard nrval and subsampling definitions. Therefore as a counter the the nrval for the Northern and North Eastern regions are reduced to 50 and subsampling is reduced to 500.

Duplicated Coordinates

As mentioned in Task 1, there are multiple accidents that has occurred at the same location. However, Ripley’s K assumes events are independent, so the multiple accidents ;exact duplicates violate this assumption by artificially inflating short-range clustering. The duplicates were retained because each record represents a genuinely distinct accident, but the resulting K-curve at very short distances (below ~1 km) should be interpreted with this limitation in mind.

Biasedness in Ripley Correction

Ripley’s isotropised edge correction is used throughout. For irregular windows in this context, the isotropised correction has known bias at larger distances as the search circle extends further beyond the boundary. The rmax bound limits this, but some residual bias may persist near the upper end of the evaluation range.

Task 4 : Integration & Interpretation

Overview

The first-order and second-order analyses conducted in Tasks 2 and 3 examine the same accident datasets with two fundamentally different perspectives.

The first-order analysis involves a point pattern recognition, looking at where and when is the accident intensity the highest where the point pattern is treated as a realisation of a spatially and temporally varying intensity surface.

The second-order analysis instead looks if events interact with one another and at which distance, treating the point pattern as a realisation of a spatial interaction process.

Neither view alone is sufficient to characterise the accident pattern in Thailand; together, however, they provide a coherent and mutually validating picture, which will be integrated and interpreted in this section where the first order analysis will localise the pattern and the second order will characterise it.

Combined Insights

Location

From the first order STKDE, the Bangkok Metropolitan region is a dominant hotspot for accidents where the North and North east regions of Thailand have higher fatality accident rates. This is complemented with the K Function which confirms all accidents within the highlighted regions are clustered at every distance scale with the Bangkok region having the highest magnitude of clustering amongst the 3 regions from the Clark-Evans analysis. This therefore does confirm that the Bangkok Metropolitan region is a genuine multi-scale cluster which cannot be a fact confirmed alone. It also proves that both orders of analysis are highly consistent with each other where strength of clustering and direction are agreed upon across the highlighted regions.

It is to note however, that there is the possibility that some rural accidents are not reported due to the delayed rescue services even though in the assumptions of the exercise we assumed that the data collected is the true representative of Thailand’s accidents in 2022.

Seasonality

It is known that April and December are the peak timings when the accidents frequency is the highest in Thailand which is likely due these months being the primary holiday periods (Chanin,2014) in the country. January and December instead shows a shocking high fatality as compared to the other months. To note, from here it is shown that the fatality STKDE does diverge from the overall STKDE, which shows that the frequency and severity of accidents have to be considered as different spatial processes. From the K function, the spatial clusters are identified within the 3 regions of Thailand, on which highways are more at risk for accidents to occur. With these information combined, it helps the government to identify the holiday period risk zones, at and when to focus efforts to reduce the accident fatalities with greatest impacts.

Severity

The first order STKDE shows that the rural North Thailand and North Eastern region has greater fatality numbers, though the K function is weaker amongst the 3 regions though still being significant. Therefore, when combining the information, it shows that the rural fatal accidents are actually more dispersed but are still non-random from the still present clustering proven. This suggests that the government can still rely on the clustering trends to concentrate efforts on to reduce accident rates but more resources are required.

The Severity or fatality rates in the rural North Thailand and North Eastern region may be higher because of the longer emergency response timings of the emergency services. With common fact that hospitals with advanced medical equipment and resources are usually less accessible in rural areas, more time is required for the person to get emergency help where victims of the accidents in these rural areas may die before reaching the hospital or miss the golden window for treatment. Hence it is possible that some deaths in the rural accidents may not be severe as some of the accidents in the urban areas that has a higher chance to save the accident victims.

Furthermore, the North Eastern region accidents may be more dispersed due to the lower population density within the region, since North Eastern contains the most provinces within Thailand. Hence clustering may look more dispersed.

Assumptions and limitations

As mentioned in task 1, there are assumptions made for the exercise due since there are many layers of factors in reality that is difficult to apply in any type of analysis. It is now explained in detail below.

Independence of events

We do have to note that it is assumed in this exercise that the accidents are independent. However due to the chaotic road conditions and lack of road safety enforcement (ASIRT,2023) , reality shows that there is the presence of multi vehicle crashes and therefore duplicate coordinates. So it has to be noted of the inflation of clustering signals in both the Clark-Evans and K Function calculations and therefore an upper bound on the clustering strength.

Homogeneity

It is assumed on the uniformed risk of accident frequency and fatality across Thailand, but as mentioned the traffic volume and quality can vary in different parts of the country with notably differences between urban and rural roads. Therefore we have to note that the clustering shown in the analysis may have the possibility of reflecting population density variation rather than pure interactions.

Isotropy

The road Networks are linear, but we are assuming that the KDE and K function have the isotropic spread, where the true clustering is through 1D networks rather than a 2D space. Therefore the clustering may be overestimated by a small extent in short distances since the points on the same roads are artificially close in 2D coordinates.

Sampling & Edge effects

In task 3, due to the computation being too high for the code to run proper, sampling is applied to the Bangkok region (1000 subsamples) and the North with North Eastern region (500 subsamples each) which may have the impact of losing a little fine-scale clustering signals. The Ripley correction would also help but the indentity of the multi-polygon windows within the dataset itself would have some imperfections in the edge correction. The Simplification of the OWIN in task 2 also may introduce a little minor boundary distortions though it is still possible to gain major insights.

Task 6 : Public Safety Planning & Management

Recap of Analysis findings

The spatio-temporal kernel density estimates and Clark-Evans tests provide strong evidence that road traffic accidents in Thailand are not randomly distributed, and all three highlighted regions: the Bangkok Metropolitan Region (BMR), Northern Thailand, and North-Eastern Thailand show statistically significant clustering (p < 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶).

BMR has the strongest clustering (R = 0.265) and functions as a constant, accident hotspot. But the Northern and North-Eastern regions has an elevated fatality intensity despite showing dispersed patterns (R = 0.301 and R = 0.419, respectively), especially in January and December.

Accident frequency peaks in April and December, coinciding with the Songkran and New Year holidays. While fatality intensity concentrates in January and December, mainly in rural regions from the temporal analysis.

Vehicle-type profiling further shows that motorcycles account for over 45% of fatal accidents across all three regions, with pickup trucks dominating accident frequency in rural areas. Speeding is seen as the main cause in 71–79% of accidents, though it may be a recording practice rather a forensic finding.

Recommendations & Way Ahead

The findings will aid resource allocation and emergency response planning.

With the BMR as a multi-scale, statistically significant cluster, the government can have targeted infrastructure investment into areas like road redesigning, improved signages, and speed-calibration measures at specific highway segments identified in the K-function plots.

Whereas for Northern and North-Eastern Thailand, the elevated rural fatality burden suggests a need for enhanced emergency medical services and faster response times, given the association between rurality, delayed treatment, and higher death rates.

Seasonal peaks (January, April, and December) should be flagged to schedule enforcement campaigns like speed checks, DUI checkpoints, and patrols with more focus on the speed checks like building more automated police cameras to stop speeding since it is a dominating cause of accidents.

Motorcycles being the most common vehicle in fatal accidents, more policies should be enforced for mandatory helmet use, rider training, and infrastructure separation.

Interpretations requiring additional analysis

It is determined that fatality intensity is higher in rural areas may likely be due to longer emergency response times. But more analysis is required where hospital locations are needed to look at hospital access, or current road design to have a better understanding on how exactly should the roads be improved for public safety.

Recommendations like vehicle safety inspections also require additional data on traffic volume at peak hours, road conditions, enforcement density, and socio-economic factors for the best decision making.
